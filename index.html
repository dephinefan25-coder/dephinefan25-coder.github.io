My Story

At eighteen, I moved far from home to Xiamen, an island city in southern China, for college. I wanted to study economics. My Gaokao score (China's college entrance exam) put me in French instead. I started from zero, added economics as a second major, and slowly fell for the language. My favorite college memory is assistant directing The Marriage of Figaro with twenty classmates. We did everything ourselves, and almost 200 people came to watch.

French took me to Paris, where I studied at ESCP. During breaks I interned at Kingsoft, the company behind WPS Office, and at Source Code Capital, a VC firm in Beijing. There I kept meeting founders with great ideas who were still figuring out how to build their teams and run the day-to-day. That's when I started caring as much about how companies run as about what they build.

At Lenovo, I joined the Digital Transformation Office. Soon after I started, the outside developer behind our Power BI reporting left, and no one fully understood the system. I took it on and rebuilt it piece by piece. Then I went a step further and replaced the monthly slide decks for 8 leaders with automated dashboards. Within a year I was promoted, and our team was named best team 3 years in a row. Along the way, I learned that digital transformation is rarely about the tool and the technology. The real work is rethinking how processes flow and getting people aligned around them.

That lesson brought me to Seattle, where I'm now pursuing an MS in Business Analytics at the University of Washington Foster School of Business. I'm learning to use machine learning and AI, to help businesses make better decisions, and to be the person who understands both what these tools can do and what the business actually needs.

Project Management System Migration Case study

Our Digital Transformation project portfolio was managed in Anaplan, which was hitting its limits and costing a lot in licenses. As the business-side owner of the tool, I led the business side of the move to Microsoft Project Online (MSPO), from requirements to reporting, and made the process simpler along the way.

The problem

The Anaplan model was running out of room and its license was about to expire, which left a short window to move. Years of staff turnover had also left the model's logic poorly documented, so few people knew how it worked. As the person who maintained the model and trained its users, I became the bridge between the old system and the new one.

What I did

Align requirements

Gathered requirements and mapped the old system

  • Collected requirements from PMs, program creators across 9 business groups, and finance
  • Documented the fields, logic and workflows of four Anaplan modules
  • Turned both into a requirements package that IT used to design and build MSPO
Simplify the process

Redesigned program setup

  • Removed the program resource demand input and the benefit split by BU and GEO, which took PMs time but few people used
  • Added a priority field that portfolio reviews needed

Automate approvals

Designed and configured approval routing

  • Mapped every funding bucket to its finance approver
  • Set up the mapping so new programs carry their approver into MSPO automatically

AfterEach program's funding bucket names its approver, so requests go straight to the right person.
Rebuild reporting

Rebuilt the data flow end to end

  • Mapped Anaplan fields to their new MSPO fields
  • Worked with the data team to switch the data platform's source from Anaplan to MSPO
  • Updated the data paths behind the leadership Power BI dashboard and checked the numbers end to end

Every step in the chain changed, and I worked on each one, from the source system to the dashboard.

Outcome

I handed over my work when I left for my MSBA, and the new system went live soon after. Anaplan has been shut down, and the team now works in one tool instead of two.

What I took away

  • When the people who built a system are gone, clear documentation is the most valuable thing you can deliver.
  • Digital transformation is less about upgrading the system and more about rethinking the business process behind it.

Predicting Checkout Spend to Target Free Shipping Case study · UW Foster MSBA

I built a regression model that predicts how much each shopper will spend at checkout, so an online retailer can offer free shipping only to shoppers likely to spend $150 or more. My recommended Lasso model cut the typical prediction error from $42 to about $22, using only 7 of 103 possible inputs.

DecisionWho gets free shipping, which costs $8 per order
Data1,500 checkout sessions, 15 candidate features
MethodsOLS, cross-validation, Ridge, Lasso
ToolsPython, pandas, scikit-learn
$42 → $22typical prediction error per shopper
7 of 103inputs kept by the final model
72%of offers went to shoppers who really spent $150+
$104wasted-offer cost, lowest of all models tested

The problem

OmniShop wanted to spend its free-shipping budget only on shoppers who would spend at least $150. Finance assumed every shopper spends the average of about $113, a guess that is off by about $42 for a typical shopper and cannot tell a $60 shopper from a $180 one. I turned this into a regression problem: predict each shopper's spend in dollars, then use the prediction to decide who gets the offer.

What I did

Set the bar

Started from the simplest guess

  • Held back 30% of sessions as a test set and kept it untouched until the final check
  • Used the average spend as a baseline, then fit OLS on the 4 features the team trusted most
Check the errors

Found 8 orders skewing every prediction

  • A residual plot showed 8 VIP orders of $1,000+ pulling predictions up by about $9
  • Since the VIP team already serves these shoppers, I modeled standard shoppers only, which halved the error and lifted R² from 0.10 to 0.73
Avoid overfitting

Showed that more features made it worse

  • Added all 15 features one group at a time and compared training error with 5-fold cross-validation
  • Error on new shoppers was lowest at 4 inputs and rose as noise like region and session hour came in
Regularize

Let Lasso pick the real drivers

  • Tuned Ridge and Lasso inside a pipeline so scaling and encoding never leaked test data
  • Lasso zeroed out 96 of 103 inputs and kept the same top drivers the team trusted: past order value, recommended items, pages viewed and discount
  • It also dropped session length, which plain OLS gave a confusing negative weight because it moves with pages viewed
Test in dollars

Turned predictions into a business decision

  • Applied each model to the $150 rule on 448 test shoppers, 54 of whom really spent $150+, and counted wasted offers, missed high spenders and cost
ModelOffersWastedMissedWasted cost
Lasso471320$104
Full OLS521517$120
Trained with VIP orders602115$168

Recommendation

I recommended Lasso. It is nearly as accurate as the best model, uses only 7 inputs a revenue leader can understand, and wasted the least money. The trade-off is that it missed 20 of 54 high spenders, so I proposed logging every shopper after launch, tracking near-misses just under $150, and running a small holdout group to measure whether free shipping actually raises spend.

What I took away

  • A better score on training data can mean a worse model on new customers.
  • Looking at the errors, not just the score, found the 8 orders behind most of the problem.
  • Reporting results in dollars made the model choice a business decision, not only a technical one.

From the BUS AN 517 (ML/AI Methods and Applications) case study in the UW Foster MSBA. OmniShop is fictional and the data is synthetic; the analysis and recommendation are my own.
